\subsection{ARQ pays for losses it can hardly recover}
\label{sec:hand-arq}
Under the full-slot model, ARQ delivered exactly what unconfirmed reporting delivered, at \arqEightMin--\arqEightMax{} its energy on LoRaWAN with eight transmissions (\arqThreeMin--\arqThreeMax{} with three); on InWaterSense at SF7, this is \inwSevenArqE~J/day instead of \inwSevenUnconfE~J/day (modelled lifetime \inwSevenArqLife{} instead of \inwSevenUnconfLife{} years). This is the pessimistic end of a bracket. If the link may return during a gap's last slot, retries recovered \arqGainUniformMin--\arqGainUniformMax{} percentage points of InWaterSense's readings on LoRaWAN; if, in addition, gaps of up to an hour are radio-loss bursts, they recovered \arqGainBurstMin--\arqGainBurstMax{} points. Against the \arqLostPp{} points that unconfirmed reporting lost, ARQ thus recovered under a fifth in every model, at \arqRatioMin--\arqRatioMax{} the energy of unconfirmed reporting; on Weija, it recovered at most \arqGainWeijaMax{} points. With independent radio losses added, ARQ did recover them, raising the delivered share by \arqGainMin{} to \arqGainMax{} points. NB-IoT ARQ recovered nothing in any timing model because the modem reports that there is no network and the node stops retrying. LoRaWAN 1.0.4 (eight transmissions at a fixed rate) changed the price only slightly: \arqVOneZeroFour{} instead of \arqVOneZeroTwo{} at SF7. Retransmission suits the radio regime, not the long gaps that hold most of the missing data.

\subsection{Buffering buys completeness at a modest energy cost}
\label{sec:hand-buffer}
On NB-IoT, searching for a cell in every slot of a gap cost \backoffMin--\backoffMax{} the energy of backing off (the ratio grows with the share of time in gaps and with $T_\mathrm{search}$); thus, the fair NB-IoT baseline backs off. Against the fair baseline, confirmed reporting with a buffer and back-off delivered every generated reading at an energy change of \bufNbMin\% to \bufNbMax\% on NB-IoT and \bufLoMin\% to \bufLoMax\% on LoRaWAN. It was no more expensive at SF7 (\bufSevenMin\% to \bufSevenMax\%), where a confirmed success costs less than an unconfirmed report and the node stops transmitting into gaps, and more expensive at SF12 (\bufTwelveMin\% to \bufTwelveMax\%); on InWaterSense at SF7, it used \inwSevenBufE~J/day (\inwSevenBufLife{} years). This completeness rests on three assumptions, each of which was tested:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The reading must exist: With up to half of the long-gap slots reassigned to a node that was off, buffering still delivered every generated reading, but only \offExpectedInws{} of the expected readings on InWaterSense.
\item The buffer must hold the backlog: The longest backlog was \maxBufferKB~KB; 2~KB of RAM lost \ramLossTwo{} of the readings, 8~KB lost \ramLossEight, and 64~KB none, and writing every reading to serial flash~\cite{winbond2018w25q128jv} cost at most \flashJDayMax~J/day in the datasheet's worst case, against at least \radioJDayMin~J/day of radio energy.
\item The drain must leave room for live traffic: Limited to what the duty cycle leaves after one live frame per slot, the drain still delivered everything. At SF7, the longest backlog cleared in \drainSevenMaxH~h; at SF12, the median backlog cleared in \drainTwelveMedH~h, but a backlog persisted for \drainTwelveMaxD~days in InWaterSense's worst period, and readings generated while it drained arrived up to \drainTwelveLiveP~days late (95th percentile); therefore, live readings should go ahead of the backlog~\cite{ingelrest2010sensorscope}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Batching is cheaper and costs little timeliness}
\label{sec:hand-batch}
Hourly batching with buffer and back-off delivered every reading, while the fair per-reading baseline used \batchSaveMin--\batchSaveMax{} as much energy. Its share of readings within 1~h differed from that of unconfirmed reporting by only \batchWithinMin{} to \batchWithinMax{} points: under long gaps, the gaps, not the batch interval, decide how many readings arrive within the hour. The back-off cap trades energy against how long the server waits once the network is back. Probing every slot cost \kEverySlotSeven{} the energy of $K=6$~h at SF7, while raising $K$ to 24~h saved only \kSixToDaySave{} more and tripled the 90th-percentile wait (\kPninetySix{} to \kPninetyDay~h); $K=6$~h sits at this knee. A better schedule could gain little: a probing schedule fitted to each record's own gap lengths (a lognormal fitted on half of the gaps and scored on the other half, the optimal-checking problem of Barlow et al.~\cite{barlow1963optimum}) saved at most \probeSaveLoraMax{} of the design's daily energy on LoRaWAN at the same detection delay, and up to \probeSaveNbMax{} on NB-IoT (\probeSaveNbRecord{} record), a figure that rests on the unmeasured cell-search time. The price of batching is the timeliness of alarms and of the real-time picture of fast parameters, which Section~\ref{sec:design} addresses.

\subsection{Many nodes and model uncertainty}
\label{sec:hand-network}
A system outage ends for every node behind the gateway simultaneously. A discrete-event model with one EU868 gateway and up to 500 nodes that buffered through a common outage followed these rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item Uplinks on the same channel and spreading factor collide unless one is 6~dB stronger; the collision rule reproduces the pure-ALOHA success probability $e^{-2G}$ in a test.
\item The gateway demodulates at most eight uplinks simultaneously and cannot receive while it transmits.
\item The gateway acknowledges in the first receive window if its 1\% sub-band allows, otherwise in the second (869.525~MHz, SF12, 10\% sub-band), and otherwise not at all.
\item Nodes back off as in (\ref{eq:backoff}), drain with up to eight transmissions per frame, and respect their own duty cycle.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:netsim} gives the results, which showed two effects. Without randomisation, the deterministic back-off kept the nodes synchronised: from \netZeroJitterN{} nodes at SF7, almost none recovered within 72~h. With probes delayed randomly by up to an hour, every SF7 node recovered, the last within \netSevenAllMax~h, at up to \netSevenEnergyMax{} the energy of a node alone. At SF12, the gateway's acknowledgements ran out: its first-window sub-band reached its 1\% limit, up to \netTwelveNoAck{} of the received frames went unacknowledged, and after a one-day outage, only \netTwelveCleared{} of 100 nodes cleared their backlog within 72~h. This is mainly a steady-state limit of confirmed traffic in large SF12 cells~\cite{capuzzo2018confirmed,hoang2026unified}, not an effect of the outage. In a ten-day run of the same model, 100 SF12 nodes whose outage lasted only 15~min held 740--870 buffered readings from the first day to the tenth, with approximately a third of the received frames unacknowledged. After a one-day outage, the backlog of 9,600 readings fell to the same level (863) within a week. The outage added a backlog that the acknowledgement-limited cell cleared slowly; it did not create a lasting failure. With 25 and 50 nodes, the backlog returned to a few readings per node within 72~h of either outage, and with 200 nodes, it grew without bound after either. The per-node results hold for cells with tens of nodes.

With every measured energy component (the LoRaWAN and NB-IoT reports, the acknowledgement time-out, the bulk-size slope, the search power, and the host's awake power) multiplied by an independent log-uniform factor between 1/2 and 2, and the NB-IoT search time drawn between 2.2 and 60~s (\mcDraws{} draws), ARQ cost more than unconfirmed reporting on LoRaWAN in \mcArqDearer{} of the draws (a ratio of at least \mcArqLow{} in 97.5\% of them) and approximately the same on NB-IoT (median \mcNbArqMed). Hourly batching was cheaper than the fair baseline in at least \mcBatchCheapOther{} of the draws at SF7 and on NB-IoT, but only in \mcBatchCheapTwelveMin--\mcBatchCheapTwelveMax{} at SF12. The buffering energy relative to the fair baseline ranged from \mcBufLo{} to \mcBufHi{}, and its sign is set by the ratio of confirmed to unconfirmed energy, which can only be settled by measuring the target hardware.

In response to RQ2, ARQ recovers only the short radio losses under any timing model, and on LoRaWAN, it multiplies energy. Buffering with back-off recovers every generated reading, given tens of kilobytes of storage, at an energy \bufLoMin{} to \bufLoMax\% different from that of recovering nothing on LoRaWAN (\bufNbMin{} to \bufNbMax\% on NB-IoT), and hourly batching is the energy-efficient way to do it at SF7 and on NB-IoT. Many nodes recovering simultaneously need randomised probes, and large SF12 cells are limited by the gateway's acknowledgements.
