\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{R}{iver} and reservoir monitoring using in situ sensor nodes must work where field visits are expensive and power is scarce~\cite{adumanu2017survey,adumanu2020smart}. A typical node samples temperature (T), specific conductance (EC), dissolved oxygen (DO), and pH every 5--30~min and sends the readings over a cellular or low-power wide-area network (LPWAN). Such nodes lose data. The InWaterSense deployment on the Sitnica river in Kosovo missed \inwsLoss{} of its expected readings~\cite{ahmedi2022dataset}; the CSIRO water-quality network in Australia averaged approximately 64\% delivery because the gateway's connection to the server ``was down occasionally''~\cite{ledinh2007design}; and deployment reports describe gateways that lose power every night~\cite{langendoen2006murphy}, sinks that stop reconnecting until rebooted~\cite{ingelrest2010sensorscope,brzozowski2018lessons}, outages of days to weeks in a subarctic DASH7 network~\cite{puluckul2025subarctic}, and prolonged cellular outages together with a database that was offline for weeks~\cite{zennaro2025kyrgyzstan}.

A node can retransmit a lost reading until it is acknowledged (automatic repeat request, ARQ), buffer it and backfill it later, or leave the server to reconstruct it from the site's history or its neighbours. The correct response depends on how data are lost: retransmission helps against a short radio fade but wastes energy against a gateway that is down for a day, and reconstruction can fill ten minutes but not a week. Yet, the analyses behind these remedies assume short losses: estimation over lossy links uses independent or short-memory (Markov fading) packet errors~\cite{sinopoli2004kalman,huang2019retransmit,huang2020harq}, and imputation methods are evaluated on random masks or simulated failures of a few hours~\cite{kong2014dataloss,cini2022grin,marisca2022spin,chang2023deepcs}. Deployment reports describe long outages, but how long real delivery gaps last, how much of the missing data they hold, and what this implies for the energy and timeliness of each remedy have not been measured across records and compared remedy by remedy.

The contribution of this study is not a new retransmission or prediction primitive; ARQ, buffering, back-off, batching, and error-bounded (dual-prediction) reporting are established. It is an evidence-to-design chain for outage-aware, energy-efficient telemetry. Real delivery records measure how long losses last and where the missing data are. The records are replayed through a measurement-parameterised LPWAN energy model, with explicit bounds on what they cannot show (why a reading is missing, and when within a slot the network returned), to decide when each remedy pays. The resulting reporting design is fixed on one year of river data and tested on a held-out year. Four research questions structure this study:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[RQ1] In the records studied, how long are the delivery gaps, how much of the missing data do long gaps hold, and is a geometric (memoryless) gap-length model adequate?
\item[RQ2] Under these gaps, what do retransmission, buffering with back-off, and batching recover, at what energy and delay, on LoRaWAN and NB-IoT?
\item[RQ3] Can reconstruction from a site's own history or its neighbours replace delivery during a gap?
\item[RQ4] Can one reporting scheme raise alarms as fast as per-reading reporting and keep the server's real-time picture within a stated tolerance as often, at substantially less energy than per-reading retransmission, on data not used for its design?
\end{enumerate}

The contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item Empirical loss structure (Section~\ref{sec:outages}): In four records, losses fall into short radio losses and rare long delivery gaps, which hold most of the missing readings in the water-quality records; a geometric gap-length model is strongly unsupported.
\item Consequence analysis with explicit bounds (Sections~\ref{sec:handling} and~\ref{sec:reconstruct}): ARQ recovers under a fifth of the missing readings even under optimistic retry timing, at \arqRatioMin--\arqRatioMax{} the energy of unconfirmed reporting on LoRaWAN, whereas buffering with back-off recovers every generated reading at an energy \bufLoMin{} to \bufLoMax\% different from that of recovering nothing on LoRaWAN, a difference whose sign depends on the radio's confirmed-to-unconfirmed energy ratio. Of the evaluated reconstruction estimators, including two deep imputers, the best for each gap keeps only \recDayAll{} of day-long gaps within tolerance (95\% CI \recDayAllLo--\recDayAllHi).
\item An outage-aware reporting design, confirmed on held-out data (Section~\ref{sec:design}): Bounded event-driven batching combines hourly batches, immediate alarm uploads, a bounded-error trigger that mirrors the server's estimate, and buffering with back-off. It was developed at \designSites{} USGS river sites in 2023 and tested, with its rules frozen, at \confSites{} sites in 2024.
\item Limits of further protocol work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}): A probing schedule fitted to the records' own gap lengths saves at most \probeSaveLoraMax{} of the design's energy on LoRaWAN, and once readings are batched, transmission is only \txShareFinalMin--\txShareFinalMax{} of the node's energy; simple rules therefore leave little for a more elaborate reporting protocol to gain under these gaps.
\end{itemize}

Scope: The two water-quality records cannot fully separate a network outage from a node that stopped producing readings; therefore, this study speaks of delivery gaps and treats buffering's recovery as an upper bound. Energy comes from a model parameterised by published measurements, not from hardware built for this study, and the claims are ratios between strategies on the same radio.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and models. Sections~\ref{sec:outages}--\ref{sec:design} answer RQ1--RQ4, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses guidance and limitations.
