\section{A Reporting Design and its Evaluation}
\label{sec:design}

Sections~\ref{sec:outages}--\ref{sec:reconstruct} point to a design: buffer and back off through gaps, batch routine data, and show the site's own diel estimate between deliveries. Batching leaves two weaknesses: alarms and the real-time picture of fast parameters. The design closes them with two triggers that send a batch early.

\subsection{The design}
\label{sec:des-design}
Event-driven batching buffers every reading and uploads the buffer once per hour in confirmed bulk frames. When the node's event detector raises an alarm, the buffer is uploaded immediately. A failed upload puts the node into an outage mode, in which an alarm triggers an immediate probe outside the back-off schedule (\ref{eq:backoff}); Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows the behaviour in a short scenario. Before the outage, the alarm makes the event-driven node upload immediately, where plain batching would wait for the end of the hour; the alarm during the outage cannot get through; and after the outage, when the backlog arrives depends on where each strategy is in its back-off.

Bounded event-driven batching adds a bounded-error trigger (Algorithm~\ref{alg:design}). Because every upload is confirmed, the node knows which readings have reached the server and when; therefore, it can compute the value $\hat{x}_j(t)$ that the server shows for each parameter $j$ by evaluating (\ref{eq:picture}) on those readings. It uploads immediately if
\begin{equation}
\max_{j}\ \frac{|x_j(t)-\hat{x}_j(t)|}{\delta_j}>1,
\label{eq:trigger}
\end{equation}
that is, if any parameter's true reading differs from what the server shows by more than its tolerance $\delta_j$. This is the dual-prediction mechanism~\cite{dias2016survey,santini2006adaptive}, used to bound the error between batches rather than to suppress reports. Three details matter:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The trigger is not consulted in outage mode, where the estimate may drift far and probing would waste energy on a dead link; only alarms probe.
\item The node's copy of the server must model when readings arrive. The duty cycle spaces the frames of a long upload; therefore, a reading counts only after its frame's arrival, and the newest valid reading goes in the first frame.
\item The node's knowledge is only as good as its acknowledgements. On NB-IoT, the confirmation is the application's reply; on LoRaWAN, it comes from the network server, and forwarding to the application server, which UVA shows can fail, is not covered. A variant closes this gap by keeping the bound for both possible server states until an application acknowledgement returns in a later downlink (Section~\ref{sec:des-results}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Bounded event-driven batching: node, one slot}
\label{alg:design}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State append the slot's reading $x_t$ to the buffer
\If{in outage mode}
  \If{alarm at $t$ \textbf{or} back-off timer expired} \State probe; on success leave outage mode and upload the buffer \EndIf
  \State \Return
\EndIf
\State $\hat{x}(t) \gets$ server's estimate (\ref{eq:picture}), from readings whose arrival time is $\le t$
\If{alarm at $t$ \textbf{or} trigger (\ref{eq:trigger}) \textbf{or} hour boundary}
  \State upload the buffer (newest valid reading first), confirmed bulk frames
  \State on failure enter outage mode, back-off (\ref{eq:backoff})
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:des-protocol}
Two phases: The design was developed on 2023 data and tested on held-out 2024 data. In development, hypotheses H1--H4 and their decision rules were written before their runs; after H4 failed, H5--H10 were written for the bounded-error trigger before it was implemented or run. Five deviations were recorded when made:
\begin{itemize}
\item D1: UVA replayed as system outages plus radio losses.
\item D2: one site excluded because its eligibility had been computed over its record span rather than the year.
\item D3: seeds derived from site identifiers.
\item D4: two implementation defects of the packing order, which made H8 fail as written (below).
\item D5: a floating-point tie at the tolerance, found while the held-out runner was being built and before any 2024 data were analysed. The pH and T records are in 0.1 steps, and an error of exactly one tolerance was counted in or out by rounding noise, in the scores and in the trigger.
\end{itemize}
All development results are from a rerun with D5 fixed; in the first development run, H4 had also failed for pH, a failure caused by D5, not by batching. For the held-out phase, the final design, the analysis code, its decision rules, and the site-eligibility rule were frozen, and the code was identified by cryptographic hashes before any 2024 data were downloaded. The plans were kept with the code; they were not registered with a third party.

Post hoc correction: After the held-out run, an audit of the model inputs found two errors. The energy model charged sensing at the host's power with the sensor board on, omitting the temperature and conductivity circuits, and charged the board during radio attempts; DO saturation was computed at sea-level pressure, which understated DO in percent saturation by a factor of \doAltFactorMin--\doAltFactorMax{} at the high-altitude sites ($n=\doAltSites$) and thus loosened their DO tolerance. Both were corrected, and every run of both years was repeated with the frozen analysis code. No verdict changed. Table~\ref{tab:hyp} reports the verdicts of the frozen run, and every number in the text comes from the corrected runs.

Setting: The evaluation used \designSites{} sites in 2023 and \confSites{} in 2024 (\sitesBothYears{} in both), sampled every 5--60~min.
\begin{itemize}
\item Links: no outage, and the InWaterSense, Weija, and UVA records, mapped onto each site's time grid in physical time, wrapped circularly with a random start offset per site (seeded by the site identifier, with a different seed in 2024); UVA as its system outages plus independent radio losses at the rate of a representative sensor (\uvaSensorFourRadio{} per attempt). The first 20\% of each series trains the detector, and all metrics use the remaining 80\%.
\item Radios: NB-IoT, LoRaWAN SF7, and LoRaWAN SF12.
\item Strategies: unconfirmed, ARQ + buffer, hourly batching, event-driven batching, and bounded event-driven batching (L2, unless stated otherwise).
\item Events: steps and ramps of 5 and 8 robust standard deviations, lasting 3~h, injected one at a time into the real series (\designEventsConf{} in 2024), as described below. All strategies use the same causal CUSUM detector, fixed before any evaluation.
\end{itemize}

Event injection: For parameter $j$, let $a_j(t)$ be the reading minus its centred 24-h running median, and $r_j(t)$ that anomaly minus its median for the same calendar month and hour of day. The event scale is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_j=\max\left(1.4826\,\operatorname{MAD}\!\left[r_j\right],\ \rho_j\right),
\label{eq:scale}
\end{equation}
floored at the recording resolution $\rho_j$, the most common positive difference between sorted distinct values. An event of amplitude $\kappa\sigma_j$, with $\sigma_j$ from (\ref{eq:scale}), $\kappa\in\{5,8\}$, and a random sign, lasts 180~min: a step adds $\kappa\sigma_j$ throughout, and a ramp rises linearly from 0 to $\kappa\sigma_j$; values are rounded to $\rho_j$. Events touch one parameter each, lie in the test period, at least a day after the start of a data segment and wholly inside it, and are at least three days apart (at most 60 per site and setting). Their median amplitudes are mild in physical units (5$\sigma$/8$\sigma$: T \ampFiveT/\ampEightT~$^\circ$C, DO \ampFiveDO/\ampEightDO~\%sat, EC \ampFiveEC/\ampEightEC~$\mu$S/cm, pH \ampFivePh/\ampEightPh); of the 2024 events, \plausibleConf{} stay within T $-0.5$--40~$^\circ$C, positive EC, pH 4--11, and DO 0--250~\%sat (\plausibleDev{} in 2023), the latency verdicts also hold on the plausible events alone, and real 3-h changes of at least 5$\sigma$ occur at \realFiveT{} of the sites for T, \realFiveDO{} for DO, \realFiveEC{} for EC, and \realFivePh{} for pH.

Detector: The detector compares each reading with the reading $m=120$~min earlier, corrected by a learned hour-of-day profile $p(\cdot)$ of the departure from the running 24-h median, and runs a two-sided CUSUM~\cite{page1954continuous} on the clipped, standardised residual:
\begin{align}
z_t&=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{x_t-x_{t-m}-p(h_t)+p(h_{t-m})}{s},\,-c,\,c\right),\label{eq:z}\\
S^{+}_t&=\max\left(0,\,S^{+}_{t-1}+z_t-k\right),\quad S^{-}_t=\max\left(0,\,S^{-}_{t-1}-z_t-k\right),\label{eq:cusum}
\end{align}
where $h_t$ is the hour of day and $s$ is a robust residual scale floored at $\rho_j$. An alarm is raised when either sum in (\ref{eq:cusum}), driven by (\ref{eq:z}), exceeds $H$, with $k=3$, $H=8$, and $c=6$; the detector then stays in an event state until $|z_t|<2$ for 60~min. The profile and scale are initialised on the training period and updated by exponential smoothing (memory of approximately 7~days) outside the event state. These parameters were fixed before any evaluation. In 2024, the detector caught a median (over sites) of \detectStepFive{} of 5$\sigma$ steps, \detectStepEight{} of 8$\sigma$ steps, \detectRampFive{} of 5$\sigma$ ramps, and \detectRampEight{} of 8$\sigma$ ramps (\detectAll{} of all events), with a median of \faMedian{} false alarms per parameter per 30 days (mean \faMean).

The strategies were compared on three metrics:
\begin{itemize}
\item Alarm latency, from event onset to arrival at the server (detected events, link up at onset).
\item Transmission energy (\ref{eq:energy}), including alarm and false-alarm uploads.
\item The picture, i.e., the share of slots within tolerance on the series without events.
\end{itemize}
Each is the median over sites per link and radio (12 combinations; 48 with the four parameters), with 95\% confidence intervals from resampling sites. Table~\ref{tab:hyp} lists the hypotheses and verdicts.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:des-results}
Alarms (H1, H7): With the link up, event-driven batching raised alarms as fast as per-reading ARQ in both years (Table~\ref{tab:design}); the median latency of approximately an hour is the detector's own. Plain hourly batching was slower in every combination, by \batchLagMinConf--\batchLagMaxConf~h in 2024, and the bounded-error trigger did not delay the alarms. Counting all injected events, the server knew \knownOneEventNoneConf{} within an hour with event-driven batching on the no-outage link, as with per-reading reporting, against \knownOneBatchNoneConf{} with hourly batching; within a day, it knew \knownDayEventNoneConf{} with any of them. The ceiling is detection, not reporting.

Energy (H2, H6): Alarms were rare (a median of \alarmsPerDay{} per node per day); therefore, event-driven batching cost \eventOverBatchMinConf--\eventOverBatchMaxConf{} as much as plain batching. The bounded-error trigger added uploads between batches, at \boundedOverBatchMinConf--\boundedOverBatchMaxConf{} the energy of plain batching, and per-reading ARQ still used \arqOverBoundedMinConf--\arqOverBoundedMaxConf{} as much energy as the bounded design (every site-resampled 95\% interval above \arqOverBoundedCiLowConf; 2023: \arqOverBoundedMinDev--\arqOverBoundedMaxDev).

Whole node: Transmission is not the whole budget. With sensing charged at \hostSenseMW~mW for \tSenseS~s per sample, transmission was \txShareFinalMin--\txShareFinalMax{} of the bounded design's node energy and \txShareArqMin--\txShareArqMax{} of per-reading ARQ's (medians over sites). Per-reading ARQ therefore used \nodeArqOverFinalMin--\nodeArqOverFinalMax{} the bounded design's node energy, against \arqOverBoundedMinConf--\arqOverBoundedMaxConf{} its transmission energy, and the bounded design extended battery life by \lifeGainMin{} to \lifeGainMax. Once readings are batched, the reporting strategy acts on half of the energy budget or less, and the sensing time per sample matters as much as the radio.

Picture (H4, H5): Plain event-driven batching failed H4 in both years, for DO only: an estimate up to an hour old leaves the tolerance between batches, and the share of slots within tolerance differed from that of per-reading reporting by \hFourFailMinConf{} to \hFourFailMaxConf{} points. The failures occurred with no outage at all; they are a property of hourly batching. (In the first development run, H4 also failed for pH; those failures were caused by the tolerance tie, not by batching.) The bounded-error trigger removed the failure: its share differed from that of per-reading reporting by \hFiveDiffMinConf{} to \hFiveDiffMaxConf{} points for every parameter, link, and radio (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), and with the link up, it was within tolerance at every slot (H8, after the packing-order fix D4). The bound costs more at tighter tolerances, yet per-reading ARQ used \levOneArqOverMinConf--\levOneArqOverMaxConf{} the bounded design's energy at L1 and \levThreeArqOverMinConf--\levThreeArqOverMaxConf{} at L3 (H9, H10). Table~\ref{tab:picture} gives the picture for every parameter. Under the two water-quality gap records, unconfirmed reporting showed a better picture than ARQ + buffer for every parameter and radio (DO on InWaterSense at SF7: \picInwsDoUnconf{} vs \picInwsDoArq): when the link returns, an unconfirmed node delivers its next reading immediately, while a buffering node waits for its next back-off probe.

Packing order (H8): On SF12, a frame holds only a few readings (Section~\ref{sec:data-energy}); therefore, an hourly batch at 15-min sampling needs two frames, and the duty cycle delays the second. With first-in-first-out packing, the newest reading rode in the second frame, and in the first development run, the picture on the no-outage link fell to \fifoMinSfTwelve{} at worst. H8 failed as written at one site (\hEightPreSite, 5-min sampling, SF12; minimum \hEightPreMin) because of two implementation defects: the reading promoted to the first frame could be a placeholder for a sensor gap, and the node's copy of the server counted readings as known as soon as their upload began, although at 5-min sampling an SF12 upload spans several slots. With both fixed (D4), newest-first packing was within tolerance at every scored slot of the no-outage link, on every site, parameter, and radio, in both years, at \nfRatioMin--\nfRatioMax{} times the energy of first-in-first-out packing.

Acknowledgement boundary: If a LoRaWAN node may not trust the network server's acknowledgement and keeps the bound for both possible server states, the design cost \ackCostMin{} to \ackCostMax{} more energy on the development sites. Its picture stayed within \ackPicWorst{} points of per-reading reporting, and per-reading ARQ still used \ackArqOverMin--\ackArqOverMax{} as much energy.

What the hourly batch buys: Plain dual prediction, which sends only when the server's estimate would leave tolerance~\cite{dias2016survey,santini2006adaptive}, is the standard way to send less. A post hoc ablation on the held-out sites removed the hourly batch and kept everything else (the trigger, the alarm uploads, and buffering with back-off). It saved \noBatchSaveMin--\noBatchSaveMax{} of the transmission energy and changed the picture by at most \noBatchPicMaxDiff{} points, but only \noBatchHourMin--\noBatchHourMax{} of the readings then reached the server within 1~h, against \noBatchHourNoneFinal{} with the batch and no outage. The hourly batch is therefore the price of a complete record on the server within the hour; where only alarms and a bounded real-time picture are needed, the trigger alone suffices.

In response to RQ4, the scheme met every criterion with the link up, on data not used for its design. Bounded event-driven batching delivers every generated reading, raises alarms as fast as per-reading reporting, and keeps the picture within tolerance as often, while per-reading ARQ used \arqOverBoundedMinConf--\arqOverBoundedMaxConf{} as much energy. No scheme fixes the gap itself: data and events during a gap reach the server only when the link returns.
